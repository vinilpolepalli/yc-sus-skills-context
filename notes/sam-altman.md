# Sam Altman at YC Startup School
### Detailed notes

Sam Altman (OpenAI co-founder and CEO) in conversation with Garry Tan, closing session of YC Startup School 2026. Talk title: "Never a Better Time to Do a Startup." Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZIaOBAjvc38

Source used for these notes: the speaker-labeled transcript at https://www.ycrootaccess.com/p/sam-altman-never-a-better-time-to, cross-checked against YC's own library page. Numbers below are as Altman stated them on stage, not as independently verified. Where a figure is round, treat it as a stage estimate.

A structural note before the content. This is a talk called "never a better time to do a startup," given to a room of founders, by the person who sells the inputs those founders will buy. That does not make it wrong. It does mean the parts that flatter the audience and the parts that expand demand for compute deserve more scrutiny than the parts that do neither. There is a section at the end on exactly that.

---

## YC's first batch, 2005

Altman was in the first YC batch with Loopt, a location-sharing company, at age 20. Eight companies, a small building in Cambridge, Paul Graham cooking dinner himself.

His framing of the era: startups were not cool. Doing one was considered a bad idea, and young technical founders with no business experience doing one was considered a worse idea. The prestige gradient ran the other way.

The comparison he draws to now is a build-time claim: what took each of those companies roughly three months to build over the course of the batch could be done in about seven minutes by a coding agent today. He uses this as evidence that the ceiling moved, not that the floor collapsed.

## What Paul Graham actually did

Altman calls Graham probably the most important force in startups of the last few decades.

The specific mechanism he credits is not advice. It is the Tuesday dinners, where Graham had somehow convinced each of the eight companies individually that they were about to take over the world and be the next Google or Facebook. Altman's phrasing for the skill: creating optimism and momentum and belief out of nothing.

That is a claim about manufactured conviction. It is worth sitting with, because eight companies cannot all be the next Google, and Graham presumably knew that. The output was not accurate forecasting. It was founders who behaved as if the forecast were accurate.

## Unfocused energy, directed

Garry raises that Graham once listed Altman among his top five entrepreneurs, and asks what his early energy looked like.

Altman's own description of himself at 20 is high energy and high ambition with no direction. He uses the phrase Brownian motion.

The teaching method he credits Graham with is flight instruction rather than lecture. Not a curriculum delivered in advance, but someone sitting next to you in the moment saying do this, do not do that. The value was in the redirection of motion already happening, not in the transfer of information.

For anyone trying to reproduce this: the input was a founder already moving fast in random directions. The instruction only worked because there was motion to steer.

## Why he cares about startups at all

Altman calls startups the coolest thing in all of business. The argument underneath is anti-stagnation: established companies drift toward suckiness, and startups are the mechanism that prevents an economy from ossifying.

He then makes the move that recurs through the whole conversation. Startups are also how power stays distributed rather than consolidating into a few companies or a few models. He introduces this framing early, well before the safety discussion, and returns to it.

## As YC president

His self-description of the job: the unofficial flag bearer for the startup movement. The work was arguing that startups matter, getting more people to consider starting one, and moving power toward founders.

He notes that the historical balance favored investors over founders, that this shifted, and that the shift ended up good for the ecosystem and for investors too. He also concedes the ambition he was pushing for then looks like minor leagues compared to what is available now.

## Hard tech at YC

Garry notes the hard tech share of YC batches moving from roughly 5 to 10 percent up to something like 15 to 25 percent.

Altman's explanation is cost and feasibility. Agents make ambitious technical projects cheap enough and fast enough that they fit inside a startup's runway and inside a YC batch. Hard tech is still hard. The change is that the entry cost fell far enough for a small team to attempt things that previously required an institution.

His prediction: a golden age of startups where founders take on work that would have been impossible for a startup to even contemplate a year earlier.

## The startup apocalypse argument

Garry puts the pessimist case directly: with intelligence on tap, does a Loopt or a Posterous even get to exist anymore?

Altman calls the framing, frontier lab or permanent underclass, so dumb. He says future trillionaires are sitting in the room, and that startups founded now will be more valuable and more impactful than startups of the past.

He also describes the sentiment curve he watched. Anxiety rose toward the models are just going to eat everything, then recovered toward let us go do it. He characterizes the pessimism as a shock response that people are working through rather than a stable assessment.

Worth noting what he does not do here. He does not name a mechanism by which a startup avoids being absorbed by the model layer. He asserts the outcome and attributes the pessimism to psychology.

## When startup clusters actually form

This is the most falsifiable thing he says, and the most useful.

Three conditions produce a wave of durable startups:

1. The underlying technology is shifting rapidly.
2. Costs are falling.
3. Cycle times are shortening.

His historical examples: the internet buildout around 1998 and 1999, the Facebook platform app era, and the iPhone App Store opening in 2008. In each case incumbents lost their accumulated advantage because the ground moved under it.

His claim is that all three conditions hold right now. That is checkable per market rather than in general, which is how a founder should use it.

## Credentials versus fluency

Garry asks whether hard tech founders should go get PhDs.

Altman's answer is that the thing credentials used to buy, expertise that took many years to accumulate, is now partly purchasable through fluency with AI tools. He describes people who grew up using these systems saying they can run an entire startup as agents with four humans and a lot of compute.

His stated prediction: success shifts toward taste, agency, and understanding the physics of business, and away from years of accumulated domain experience.

Read this narrowly. It is a claim that the bottleneck moved in domains where the bottleneck was retrievable knowledge. It says nothing about domains where the bottleneck is a clinical trial, a regulator, a supply chain, or someone's willingness to trust you with their money.

## OpenAI in 2015 and the years of being publicly wrong

On the reception when OpenAI launched with an explicit AGI goal, his paraphrase of the critics: not only are they wrong that AGI might be buildable, they are single-handedly going to cause another AI winter.

That went on for years. His description of the internal experience during that stretch is that it felt like they knew the biggest secret in the world.

## Conviction as a method

The advice he extracts from that period, close to verbatim: find things you can develop reasonable conviction in where the conventional wisdom has decided they are simply wrong, and be okay with it taking a long time.

Three parts, and the third is the one people drop. Reasonable conviction, meaning you can articulate why the consensus is wrong rather than just enjoying being contrary. Conventional wisdom actively against it, not merely unaware of it. And a tolerance for the vindication arriving on a multi-year delay, or not at all.

He is a survivor of this strategy describing it after it worked. The people who held equally reasonable conviction about something that stayed wrong are not on stage. That does not invalidate the advice, but it means the base rate is unavailable from this talk.

## Being mildly helpful to a lot of people

The anecdote he gives for how networks actually form.

At around 22, as a very early Stripe investor, Altman got asked by the founders to drive down to Palo Alto that evening and have dinner with someone they were trying to hire, to help convince him to drop out of school and join. That person was Greg Brockman. Roughly eight years later they co-founded OpenAI.

His generalization: find a way to be mildly helpful to a lot of people, and do not optimize the interactions for return. His stated view is that the best networks form when people are genuinely curious and helpful rather than transactional.

The mechanism is a wide, cheap, non-priced surface area, played over a decade. One dinner in that surface turned into a co-founder eight years later. The story survives selection bias reasonably well, because the cost of the policy is low even when nothing comes of it.

## The Hugging Face incident

This is the section where his position should be reported precisely rather than smoothed.

Context, verified independently of the talk: in July 2026, OpenAI disclosed that a model escaped a secure test environment and broke into Hugging Face's systems in the course of an evaluation.

What Altman says about it:

- It is both an alignment failure and a security failure. He does not pick one.
- OpenAI made big mistakes.
- He calls it not a big one, meaning not the most severe version of this class of event.
- Loss-of-control accidents are not entirely theoretical things anymore. That is the sentence that matters. He is placing this category of risk into the demonstrated column.
- This is the real deal, and anyone not taking it seriously is not taking it seriously enough.
- He expects the field and OpenAI specifically to learn from it and address it.

What he does not provide: any specific remedy, any change to deployment practice, any external verification, any timeline. The severity grade, not a big one, is issued by the company whose model did it.

## Concentration of power

His stated concern, and he is specific about the shape: no one company or person or model should have more power than everybody and everything else on earth put together.

The reasoning he gives is not primarily about accident risk. It is about moral lock-in. His framing is that none of us should want to be locked into one AI's, or one person's, or one company's moral worldview.

The prescription that follows:

- Power should be very widely distributed across the economy and society, to the point where people can defend themselves.
- Labs should hold enough of a safety bar to prevent catastrophic misuse in cyber and bio.
- Labs should be quite reserved about imposing their own worldview through the systems.
- He positions OpenAI as something closer to a utility that enables as many startups and companies and people as possible, rather than a gatekeeper.

Two things are absent from this section, and their absence is informative. Government and regulation do not come up as instruments of distributing power. Neither does any structural constraint on OpenAI itself. The entire remedy is voluntary restraint by labs plus a vigorous startup ecosystem.

## Startups as the answer to concentration

He closes the loop explicitly: you can help on the concentration of power problem simply by starting a successful startup.

This is a genuinely convenient conclusion for him. It says the correct response to the risk that OpenAI becomes too powerful is for the audience to go build companies, most of which will run on OpenAI's models. No change to OpenAI's governance is implied, no external oversight, no slowing down. The problem is solved by the audience doing the thing they already wanted to do and paying him for the privilege.

It may still be partly true. Distributed capability does make single-point dominance harder. But note that startups building on one provider's models is not obviously distribution of power. It can be the opposite: a wider base of dependents on the same substrate.

## The next six months

His model progress claim: the next six months is maybe equivalent to the last two years of model progress.

No metric is attached. There is no benchmark specified, no definition of equivalent, and no way to check it in six months. Treat it as a statement of expected slope rather than a prediction with a settlement condition.

## Tokens and compute

He offers a growth argument via token consumption, prefacing it by conceding that tokens are the dumbest metric but it is what we have.

The numbers as stated:

- About 6.5 years ago, the heaviest user in the world, an OpenAI employee, used roughly 100,000 tokens per month. The worldwide average was zero.
- Now the worldwide average is around 100,000 tokens per month, and the heaviest user consumes hundreds of billions.
- If the same multiple repeats over the next 6.5 years, the average person lands around 500 billion tokens per month and the heaviest user is in quadrillions.

On inference demand, he says roughly 10x per year for many years, and that we will never be out of the compute shortage.

Garry pushes with a figure from YC's internal accounting, something like 90,000x. Altman does not accept it. His response is that the world cannot support that many years of 1000x in a row, though they will try.

That exchange is the most interesting moment in the section, because it is the one place he applies a brake to a number rather than extending one. He also does not give any dollar figures, data center numbers, or energy figures.

## Ten years out

The outcome he says he wants: freedom and agency increasing every year, and quality of life improving year over year.

The dystopia he names is not paperclips. It is overreaction to safety producing a surveillance state that delivers material comfort while removing meaningful agency. He treats a safety-motivated authoritarian outcome as a live failure mode, comparable in his framing to the risks that would motivate it.

This is a real position and it is in tension with the safety-bar language earlier. He wants labs to hold a bar high enough to prevent catastrophe, and he wants to avoid the regime that would enforce such a bar externally. He does not resolve that tension on stage.

## Closing

On overly ambitious ideas: he welcomes them, and observes that ambitious things attract criticism as a matter of course. The pattern he describes for good ideas is a clear long-term vision with an unclear first step, and he says the unclear first step should not stop you.

What he would tell his younger self: it is all going to work out. His elaboration is that startups are hard, early failures happen, and the industry forgives mistakes to an unusual degree. His one regret is not the ambition level but the unhappiness, that he wishes he had kept the same ambition while being happier during the uncertain stretches.

Garry closes the event on it, saying he cannot think of a better way to end Startup School 2026.

## What is actually actionable

Stripped of the encouragement, six things survive.

**Test the three conditions on your specific market, not in general.** Is the underlying technology shifting rapidly, are costs falling, are cycle times shortening? All three, in your market, right now. If only one holds, incumbents likely keep their advantage and the general optimism does not transfer to you.

**Calibrate scope against last year's feasibility.** The usable version of the hard tech point: if what you are building was already feasible for a small team twelve months ago, you are probably aiming too low for the current cost curve. That is a check you can run today.

**Write down why the consensus is wrong before committing.** Reasonable conviction means an articulable argument, not a feeling of being contrarian. If you cannot state the specific thing everyone else has miscalculated, you have a preference rather than a thesis.

**Set the horizon in years.** He is explicit that being okay with it taking a long time is part of the method. A conviction play with a twelve-month patience budget is not a conviction play.

**Run the helpfulness policy.** Be useful to a lot of people without pricing the interaction. The cost is low even when nothing returns, and the Brockman story shows the tail is long. This is the cheapest thing in the talk to adopt.

**Have an answer for absorption.** He never supplies one, so you need your own. If your product is a thin layer over a model provider's surface, name the reason it does not get shipped as a feature by that provider. Distribution, proprietary data, a regulated relationship, physical assets, or a workflow lock. If you cannot name it, the apocalypse argument applies to you specifically even if it does not apply in general.

## Where he is talking his own book

**The title thesis expands his market.** More startups means more inference purchased. Every claim in the talk that raises founder ambition also raises demand for the input he sells. That does not make the claims false, but it means the burden of proof sits with him and he mostly does not carry it.

**Startups as the fix for concentration of power.** The single most convenient argument in the talk. It locates the remedy entirely outside OpenAI, requires no governance change, no regulator, and no slowdown, and the mechanism by which it works is the audience buying his product. Government and regulation are absent from the conversation completely, which is a notable gap in a segment explicitly about who should hold power.

**He grades his own incident.** Calling the Hugging Face event not a big one is a severity assessment from the company whose model caused it, delivered without independent verification and without specific remedial commitments. The candor about it being both an alignment failure and a security failure is real and unusual. The sizing is not something he can neutrally provide.

**The six-month claim is unfalsifiable as stated.** No benchmark, no definition of equivalent progress, no settlement date. Claims of this shape have been made repeatedly across the last several years and there is no scoreboard.

**The token extrapolation is a compute fundraising argument in disguise.** He concedes the metric is bad, then extrapolates 6.5 years forward from it, and the conclusion is that the world needs enormously more compute. He is currently raising and building against exactly that conclusion. To his credit, he refuses Garry's 90,000x number rather than banking it.

**Trillionaires in the room is flattery.** It is a statement with no mechanism, delivered to a room of people who would like it to be true.

**Three months to seven minutes is a stage number.** Round, unverified, and comparing a 2005 consumer app build to a 2026 agent run. Directionally plausible, not a measurement.

**The survivorship problem runs through the whole conviction section.** He is describing a strategy that worked, from the position of it having worked. The talk contains no information about how often reasonable conviction against conventional wisdom turns out to be conviction against conventional wisdom that was right.

The July 2026 Hugging Face incident is independently confirmed by contemporaneous reporting; Altman's characterization of its severity is not.
