# Garry Tan at YC Startup School
### Detailed notes

A talk in two halves. The first is a how-to for building a personal agent system. The second is an argument about who owns your cognition, framed through Spinoza. The Spinoza material is not decoration; the whole political thesis rests on it.

Transcript garbles worth fixing before reading: Baruch Spinoza (not Baroo Spininoza), conatus (not canadus or kannotus), Gottfried Leibniz (not Gotfrieded Leenes), Sephardic, guilders, Heidelberg, Vannevar Bush, Codex. One company name comes through as "Retail winter 24" and is probably Retool or something similar.

---

## The opening: Spinoza

Tan opens by noting the internet calls him one of the most AI psychotic people online, then pivots to the most cancelled man in history.

The Einstein hook. In 1929 a New York rabbi telegrammed Einstein asking whether he believed in God, answer in 50 words. Einstein used 25: he believed in Spinoza's God, who reveals himself in the lawful harmony of the world, not a God concerned with the fate and doings of mankind. The most famous scientist alive, asked the biggest question there is, pointed at Spinoza.

The excommunication. Amsterdam, July 27, 1656. Spinoza is 23, a member of a tight-knit Sephardic Jewish community. The elders issue the most violent curse the community ever produced. Cursed by day and by night, lying down and rising up. Nobody may speak to him, trade with him, come within four cubits of him, or read anything he writes. Uniquely among roughly 40 bans that community issued that century, it carries no repentance clause. It has never been lifted and technically remains in force.

The charge was evil opinions, meaning forbidden thoughts. The sentence was complete deletion from the community.

The bribe. Before the curse, they offered him a thousand guilders a year, serious money, to show up at synagogue occasionally and keep quiet. He refused, saying not for ten thousand, that he wanted truth rather than comfort. Tan's translation into founder terms: they offered him a salary to stop building.

The knife. Shortly before the excommunication a fanatic attacked him. The blade tore his cloak and missed. He kept the cloak, unmended, for the rest of his life, to remember what ideas cost.

The work. He ground lenses by day, making optical instruments good enough that the best scientists in Europe sought them out, and by night wrote a book too dangerous to publish while alive. He died at 44 with lungs full of glass dust from making other people's lenses. The manuscript was locked in his writing desk. His dying instruction was to ship the desk by canal barge to his publisher in Amsterdam. It became the Opera Posthuma and drew immediate attention across Europe.

Conatus. Spinoza's word for the engine that kept him going. The striving in every living thing to keep going and increase its power to act. Not your resume, title, or job. The striving itself. Tan frames the whole talk as being about tools that amplify it.

---

## The updated heresy

Spinoza's actual heresy: God is not a king on a throne but spread through everything that exists. God, or nature.

Tan's version, 400 years on. Everyone is waiting for AGI as a singular event, a god in a data center, an announcement, a day the sky changes color. Everyone is watching the sky and the thing they are watching for is already in the room. It does not look like a god. It looks like infrastructure, a terminal window, a folder of markdown files, a job that finishes while you sleep. Diffused through everything, which is exactly where Spinoza said to look.

---

## Personal AGI, defined precisely

Not artificial general intelligence for everyone at once. General intelligence for one person.

He cites Vannevar Bush's Memex as the antecedent, a machine that would be an extension of yourself and your brain.

He is deliberate about what he does not mean, because the phrase personal AI has been captured by marketing. Not a chatbot you pay $20 a month for. Not slightly better autocomplete. Not an assistant that knows your calendar and nothing else. That is a subscription you rent, a corporate AGI you do not own. It resets when you close the tab. It knows what everyone else already knows. When the company pivots, your assistant gets a lobotomy on someone else's schedule.

What he does mean: an agent running on your infrastructure, reading a memory you own, executing procedures you wrote, compounding.

The asymmetry that carries the argument. Corporate AGI improves only when the company ships. Personal AGI improves every day you use it, because every day it knows more of your life. One is a product you consume. The other is an asset you build.

Almost nobody has the second thing. Everyone in the room could have it by Monday. His line: if you build this for yourself, 2034 does not have to be like 1984.

---

## Why now, and the productivity numbers

In 2013 he was a YC partner building Bookface, YC's internal social network, at night. He shipped maybe 14 useful lines of code a day, which he notes is dead on the median in the programmer productivity literature. That was him at full effort.

This year he runs YC full-time with a 5pm kid pickup and puts his output at roughly 400x 2013.

He then deflates his own number, which is the right instinct. Apply the most pathological verbosity penalty you can stomach. Assume the agent writes bloated code. Assume half is scaffolding. Assume he is flattering himself. It is still 8x at the absolute floor and about ten times that in the middle of the range.

The generalization: this is not only coding. It applies to design, product management, growth, every part of knowledge work. If you are early in your career, that is the good news.

Portfolio-scale evidence. In the Winter 25 batch, a quarter of the companies had codebases that were 95% AI generated. Those companies now use agents for everything, not just code. He says that batch is on track to be one of the fastest growing and most profitable in YC history.

He is careful about causation and says so explicitly. He cannot prove the AI-generated code caused the growth. What he claims is narrower: the fastest growing founders they fund are not treating AI as autocomplete, they are treating it as a workforce.

The framing that matters most here. There are 2x people and 100x people using the same Claude. Same weights, same context window, same API. The leverage is not in the weights. It is in what context you give it, how relevant that context is, and whether it arrives at the right step.

---

## Joy and sadness, technically

From the Ethics, joy is the feeling of your power of acting increasing. Which is why the first time an agent does a week of your work in an afternoon it does not feel like convenience, it feels like joy. Tan insists this is the technical term, not poetry. Your conatus just got bigger.

The opposite is sadness, the feeling of your power of acting decreasing. He points at the specific heaviness of Sunday nights, the sense that your ability to influence the world is receding, quiet quitting. He tells the audience to hold that thought because it comes back and gets political.

---

## The equation

A frontier model, which is rented, commoditized, and cheaper every quarter, plus your context, which is owned and ideally unique to you, plus a harness that wires them together. The harness might be OpenClaw, Hermes agent, Claude Code, or Codex.

That sum gives you an agent that acts like a very fast version of you.

His compression: model quality is rented, your brain is owned.

The lineage he places it in. McLuhan said technology is an extension of man. Jobs called the computer a bicycle for the mind. This, he says, is a self-driving rocket.

On Paul Graham's two rules. Make something people want and do things that don't scale both still govern. What is new is the multiplier on the second. Agents are how one founder does unscalable things at scale. The advice did not change; the physics did.

---

## Working memory, and why it explains everything

Humans hold about seven things at once, seven plus or minus two, from the most famous paper in cognitive psychology. It is why local phone numbers are seven digits and why you forget the eighth item on a grocery list.

His strongest structural claim follows. Every institution humanity has ever built, every checklist, org chart, filing cabinet, and standup meeting, is a prosthetic for that limit.

An agent holds a million tokens. About a thousand pages. Three Harry Potter books open on the desk at once, able to find a needle in any of them and synthesize across all three in seconds. Three books versus seven digits. Maybe not AGI, but already a different operating regime. Almost everyone on earth is still running their life on structures designed for the seven-digit brain.

Then he runs the number the other way, which is the part people miss. A thousand pages is also very little. Your life is not three books, it is a library. Every email, every meeting, every decision and the reason behind it, every conversation with every person you know.

So the question that determines whether your agent is a genius or a goldfish: who or what decides which three books are open on the desk.

That is the librarian. The brain is the library plus the librarian.

---

## GBrain

Built in the open. His personal OpenClaw runs a Karpathy-style knowledge wiki of about 220,000 markdown pages, 25 years of his life diarized. Email, meetings, notes, photos, drafts, and the things he got wrong. Compiled mostly by agents, curated by agents, searched by agents.

The claim he cares about: he never re-asks a question he already answered.

The lived example. A founder emails about a crisis. Before he finishes reading, his agent has pulled every prior conversation with that founder, three portfolio companies that hit the same wall, and what actually worked for them. When the agent does anything, it does it knowing everything he knows. That, he says, is the difference between an assistant and a colleague.

### An actual day

Overnight, the agent processes his inbox rather than sorting it. It distinguishes founders in trouble from people selling something from the seventeen mailing lists he never unsubscribed from. The ones that matter get triaged with context pulled from the library: who this person is, the whole history, what they are really asking underneath what they wrote, and what it might mean for him. He wakes to a briefing rather than a pile.

Before every meeting, a prep doc: who he is meeting, what was said last time, what changed since, what he should ask.

Research he was curious about at midnight is finished by morning.

When something interesting happens in the world, the agent has read it, cross-referenced it against what he cares about, and filed it before coffee.

### GStack

The agent coding framework sitting on top of the library. He cites 123,000 GitHub stars, which he says puts it in the top 100 open source projects in GitHub history.

The punchline about the architecture: it is mostly skill files plus a browser the agents can drive. Pages of English and a way to act on the world. Markdown, not magic. Fat skills, thin harness.

---

## What a skill file actually is

He shows a real one, lightly redacted, to demonstrate how unmagical it is. Paraphrased:

When a meeting recording lands from Circleback, transcribe it with speaker labels. Pull out the commitments made, who made them, and the deadlines. Cross-check every person named against the library and link their pages. File the summary here and the full transcript there. If anything contradicts something we already believe, flag it, do not override it.

That is the whole thing. A page of English. Anyone who can read could follow it.

The test he offers: if a smart intern could follow it, an agent can run it.

Which produces the claim he says he caught flack for and believes more than ever. Markdown is code. If you can write clear instructions in English, you are a programmer. The compiler is a language model.

The consequence is that this is not just for engineers. At YC, media people, event staff, and finance people who never opened a terminal are building skill files and scheduled jobs. One finance person compiled roughly a hundred Excel workbooks into a single app she built with an internal agent. She is not a programmer. She is a manager of agents. Everyone is about to be.

## Latent space versus deterministic space

The most technically useful section of the talk, and the one worth stealing regardless of what you think of the rest.

The question to ask is where the computation is happening. There are exactly two answers, and confusing them causes every agent failure he has seen.

Some computation belongs in latent space. Taste, judgment, reading what a human actually wants from a vague request. That lives in the model and you steer it with a markdown file.

Some computation belongs in deterministic space. Arithmetic, SQL queries, the seating chart. That needs to live in a database that the markdown files call.

His example scales the point. Seat five people around a table and either an agent or a human can do it in latent space. Build custom schedules for 6,000 people in an arena, which is what they did for that conference, and your latent-space agent needs to write code to keep track. The audience's own conference experience was markdown files calling code.

The formulation to remember: the model fails where we fail, and the fix is having the model compute the way humans compute. Latent and deterministic, markdown files calling databases and scripts.

## The compendium skill

His receipt, and the best demonstration in the talk because the audience was sitting inside the output.

Five days before the talk he decided it needed Spinoza. His agent acquired three of the best biographies, by Nadler, Goldstein, and Stewart, roughly 1,500 pages. It read all three. It produced a synthesis, a dated chronology of Spinoza's life, every point where the three biographers disagree, and the best verbatim quotes with chapter citations. Because it knows what he needs, it also ranked the ten most tellable moments with delivery notes.

The knife attack, the bribe, and the desk all came out of that overnight run. Fifteen hundred pages became a stage-ready story he could edit.

He calls this a compendium skill, uses it daily, and describes it as a much deeper version of deep research than corporate AI products will give you.

## How the library started

Not 220,000 pages. A folder. A few markdown files about the companies he was working with and the people he kept emailing.

It got big the way anything gets big, a little every day, compounding, with agents doing the filing.

Nobody builds the warehouse first. First you build one shelf.

## You are already running an organization

When you sit down with an agent tonight you are not coding, you are managing a workforce made of markdown.

A skill file is an employee: one capability, one job, written down clearly enough that someone new could execute it.

A resolver is an org chart: a task comes in and it decides which file handles it.

Which means that before you incorporate anything, before you have a co-founder or a logo or a deck, you are already running an organization of one plus your agents. You are the founder and the entire management layer, and the headcount under you is whatever you decide it is.

## The new revenue-per-person math

Two examples he gives as claims:

Emergent, from the Summer 24 batch, went from public launch to nine figures of revenue in eight months. When they crossed $15 million annualized they were 15 people.

A Winter 24 company (name garbled in the transcript) hit $60 million annualized with about 40 people.

His point is that this revenue per person did not exist before, not in software, not in oil, not in railroads. These are not freaks of nature but the first companies built natively on the new physics, and each started as one or two people wired the way he described.

The competitive framing: picture the batch room in Dogpatch, hundreds of founders each doing what used to be a person's entire year of work. That is not the future, that is the current bar. If you are not doing it, your competitor is, and they will eat your lunch politely and thank you for it.

---

## What software becomes

Software does not have to be precious anymore. You can build exactly the tool you need for an audience of one in a weekend.

The old advice was to scratch your own itch and hope it is the market. The new version is to scratch your own itch because scratching itches is nearly free. Some of your tools for one turn out to be companies, and you will know because other people start begging for them.

---

## The caveat he raises on himself

A brain nobody curates is a garbage dump with great search.

Retrieval will surface a stale fact with total confidence. A bad skill file encodes a bad process forever.

So the primitive is memory plus hygiene. Provenance on every fact. Contradiction checks when new information collides with old. A librarian whose actual job is pruning.

Treat the brain like production infrastructure and it compounds. Treat it like a dumping ground and you get a very confident agent that is wrong in ways nobody can trace.

## The five steps

Step one, tonight. Pick a harness and run an agent on your own machine. He uses OpenClaw and Hermes agent with GBrain; a hosted version is free at gbrain.io and GBrain is free and open source. His own caveat: he always recommends the Ferrari, but the Honda is really good too, and Codex or Claude Code will do 99% of this with less time spent on the roadside fixing it. The concepts are the point, not any given repo.

Step two, this weekend. Start your library. Not a grand archive, one folder of markdown files. Export your notes and your email if you can. Write one page about each project you are working on and each person you work with, containing what you are building together, what they care about, what you owe them, what they said last time. No model on earth has this, because it exists only in your head, and your head holds seven things. The first time an agent answers using your context instead of the internet's, you feel the click and you do not go back. You are sitting on five to ten years of your own history in an inbox, a moat lying there unindexed. The gate between you and this is roughly 24 hours.

Step three. Write your first skill file. Choosing it is easy: the task you do every week that you hate most. Expense reports, meeting notes, the weekly status update, competitor research. Explain it in plain English the way you would to a smart friend on their first day. Let it get things wrong and correct it. Every rule, every exception, every "oh and also" goes in. That page is now an employee. Run it.

Step four. Wire it to a recurring job. Every morning at 7, do this. Every Friday, summarize that. The first time you wake up to work that finished while you slept, something shifts permanently. The day stops being the unit of work and the unit becomes what you can imagine, driven by your goals and what you want to create.

Step five, the discipline separating compounders from dabblers. Never do one-off work. Most people run one operation with one agent, throw the context away, and close the window. Instead, at the end of every task, ask the agent to skillify what it did. Skillify is itself a skill in GBrain; you can point an agent at that repo and tell it to extract skillify and learn how. Turn the work into a markdown file you reuse forever. His YC formulation: if you have to ask for something twice, you failed. The person who captures what they learn gets smarter every day. The person who wakes up every morning with amnesia is wasting their time. It does not matter how good the model gets if you cannot turn it into real memory.

---

## The 90-day curve

Week one it is a toy. The library is thin, the skills are clumsy, you are fixing more than you are saving.

Week four the flywheel catches. The agent starts answering with your context, the morning job produces something you actually read, and you write your third and fourth skill because the first two worked.

Week twelve you have a library that answers before you finish asking, a dozen skill files running the parts of your week you used to dread, and one or two tools other people keep asking to borrow, which in that room is called a startup.

Flat, flat, flat, then not. Most people quit in week two, which is precisely why the ones who do not feel like they are cheating by week twelve.

## The political half

This is where the talk stops being a tutorial.

A skill file is not a document. It is a piece of your cognition, how you do the thing, extracted from your head, written down, and executable. Every skill you teach an agent is you externalized.

The same file is two opposite futures depending on one variable: who controls it.

### Maya

A fictional support engineer. Over two years she teaches her agents 40 skills. How to triage a P0 at two in the morning. How to de-escalate a customer about to churn. How to write a postmortem that actually prevents the next incident. Forty files, containing her judgment, which took two years to build, sitting on a disk.

Version one. The files live in Maya's repo. She changes jobs and they go with her. Day one at the new company she is operating with years of compounded judgment on tap, and every year she works she compounds further. That is ownership. If she wanted to start a company on that expertise, she could. Tan's aside: entire startups these days will be markdown files.

Version two. The files live in the company's repo under the company's IT policy. Maya leaves with nothing. The company keeps running her judgment without her, forty files executing forever, and her name is not even in the commit history. She did not have a career. She had an extraction.

Same files, same person, one variable.

### The doctrine

Skill files are yours. Own your skills, because if you do not, your job becomes a skill file.

The historical parallel. Craftsmen owned their tools, and that is what made them free. The factory broke that; the loom belonged to the mill. Knowledge workers assumed they were safe because their tools lived in their heads where nobody could confiscate them. Skill files end that. For the first time in history your cognition can be extracted, stored, versioned, and owned. The only question is by whom.

The thousand guilders never went away, it got rebranded. Every comfortable arrangement where your judgment compounds in someone else's repo is a thousand guilders a year to show up, keep quiet, and stop building your own thing. Which, he says, is why you should start a startup.

### Spinoza declines the acquisition

In 1673 Heidelberg offered the cursed heretic a full professorship: salary, legitimacy, a chair, and freedom of philosophizing, provided he not disturb the established religion.

His answer was that he did not know what the limits of that freedom of philosophizing might have to be. He read the terms of service and declined.

Spinoza's phrase for what he was protecting was being under your own power rather than under someone else's. Your power of acting exists either way. The political question in 1673 and in 2026 is who commands it.

The thesis in one sentence: personal AGI is how you stay under your own power in the age of agents. The practical instruction: keep your brain and your skills in a repo you control from day one, before any platform or acquirer has an opinion about it.

The estate inventory. When Spinoza died they catalogued the room. Two pairs of pants, seven shirts, a lens lathe, 160 books, and the Ethics locked in a desk. He owned almost nothing, and nobody ever controlled his skill files. The desk drawer was his repo.

## Three objections

The models will improve so fast that harness work becomes obsolete, so just wait for the next release. He credits this as the bitter lesson position and says he loves the people making it. His answer: the better models get, the more the differentiator moves to context. When everyone's engine is a thousand horsepower, the race is won on the driver and the map. The weights are everyone's; the library is yours. A better model makes your library worth more, because a smarter reader extracts more from the same books. Every release the labs ship is a free upgrade to a workforce you already own.

Is this just RAG? His answer: sure, and Postgres is just B-trees. Retrieval is the primitive, not the product. The hard part is everything around it. What gets written down in the first place, how it gets enriched and linked, what gets promoted to hot memory versus filed as cold reference, and who arbitrates when two facts disagree. Retrieval is easy. Being worth retrieving from is the product.

What happens when your whole life in one system leaks? He calls this the objection that deserves the most respect, and answers it with the thesis. That is exactly why it has to be yours. His brain runs on his own infrastructure, in his own repo, under his own keys. The default is not privacy; the default is your life already scattered across ten clouds owned by companies whose incentives are not yours, searchable by everyone except you. He did not create the risk by consolidating his context, he took custody of it. Custody is the security model. And if you do not trust yourself to hold the keys, the answer is not to trust someone else's terms of service more.

## Why he open sourced it

The easy answer is that he can, because being at YC means he does not have to monetize his own infrastructure.

But he says that answers the wrong question. The real question is why anyone should.

His answer is that tools of the powerful should be given away. Every era has a private technology of leverage, a thing the powerful have and everyone else does not. For a long time it was literacy. Then capital. Right now it is the harness, the library, the workforce made of markdown. The people who have it operate at a different scale than the people who do not, and the gap widens every month.

When something that powerful stays private you get a priesthood. When it is given away you get a renaissance.

His creed, offered verbatim: say the things other people won't, fund the people other people won't, build the buildings other people won't, write and give away the code that other people won't, leave behind the institutions that other people won't.

## The Leibniz chapter, on building in public

November 1676. Leibniz, the most glittering genius in Europe, silk stockings, a calculating machine in his luggage, travels to The Hague to spend three days in an attic with the most hated man on the continent.

Then he spends the next 40 years publicly lying about it, calling the visit a few hours in passing. Privately his notes are crammed with obsessive commentary on Spinoza.

Tan says he lives a small version of this weekly. He says agents write most of his code and the dunks arrive by lunch. Then he looks at what the loudest dunkers are actually shipping and it is agents all the way down.

The pattern to expect if you build in public: first they quote tweet you, then they clone you. The dunks are the adoption curve announcing itself.

## The epilepsy story

A friend whose son has a rare form of epilepsy. No lab, no grant, no permission.

He built a repo of 80,000 markdown files, a brain for one small boy, and pushed himself to the edge of what humanity knows about that exact condition. Every specialist visit, every paper, every seizure log, every drug interaction, indexed and cross-linked, so that when a new doctor proposes an idea he knows within minutes whether it has already been tried.

A father, a laptop, and a library. Tan calls this personal AGI, not a benchmark and not a demo. The whole architecture aimed at the one thing a man loves most.

Nobody was coming to build that for him, so he built it. Nobody is coming to build yours either.

## Closing

Everything you were told you needed, the team, the funding, the permission, the credential, was a workaround for the fact that one person could hold seven things in their head and work sixteen hours a day. That fact just expired.

You can fly now, not metaphorically but mechanically. Every time you thought you wished you could hire someone and couldn't, now you can. Every archive too big to read, every dataset too gnarly to clean, every ocean you were told not to boil. We can boil the ocean.

The sentence he lives by: it's all made up, but you get to make it up. Every institution in the world, including the one that read a curse over a 23-year-old in 1656, was made up by people no smarter than you.

The difference between this generation and every previous one is that they had to recruit dozens of believers before they could build anything at all. You need a laptop and a few years of your own history that you are already sitting on.

There were about 7,000 people at the event. Seven thousand conatuses, seven thousand strivings. For most of history almost all of that striving never got an audience; it died waiting for funding, headcount, permission, or someone else to believe first. He calls this the first technology he has seen that lets the striving go straight to work, with no intermediaries and no permission.

The Ethics closes with nine words: all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare. The difficulty just collapsed. The rarity is up to you.

## My read on what is actually actionable

The latent versus deterministic split is the single most useful idea in the talk and the most portable. It is a debugging heuristic you can apply immediately: when an agent fails, ask which space the failing computation was in and whether it belonged there. Arithmetic and state tracking in latent space is the failure, and the fix is markdown that calls code.

Skillify, or never doing one-off work, is the operational discipline that turns usage into an asset. It is also the only step in the five that most people will skip, and it is the one that makes the rest compound.

Library plus librarian is the right correction to naive retrieval, and his own objection-two answer is honest about where the work actually is. What gets written down and what gets promoted to hot memory matter more than the retrieval mechanism.

The curation caveat deserves more weight than its length in the talk. Provenance, contradiction checks, and pruning are the difference between a compounding asset and a confident wrong answer nobody can trace. He says this in one paragraph and then moves on, but it is the failure mode most people will hit around week six.

## Where he is talking his own book

He runs YC and is promoting GBrain and GStack, both his own projects, so read the numbers accordingly.

The 400x figure is self-reported and measured in lines of code, which is a famously poor productivity metric. He deflates the raw number honestly, but his 8x floor is still derived from the same metric rather than from output that anyone else evaluated.

The Winter 25 batch claim is explicitly flagged by him as correlational, which is to his credit. It is worth adding that YC selects for fast-growing companies in the first place, so the sample is not neutral.

The 123,000 stars and top-100-in-GitHub-history claims are stated without a citation, and stars measure attention rather than production use.

The 220,000 pages and 25 years diarized are unverifiable from a talk, and the relevant question is whether the marginal page helps. That is his own curation caveat pointed back at him.

The Maya argument is genuinely important and also, conveniently, a recruiting pitch for starting a company, which is his job.

## How this sits against the other two talks

All three speakers independently tell you to ignore the influencer layer, which is a small but real signal.

On scaffolding, Tan is the maximalist. Fat skills, thin harness, 220,000 pages, skillify everything, never throw context away. Alexandr Wang agrees, describing the mechanics as skills, markdown files, cron jobs, and a metric.

Boris Cherny argues close to the opposite: delete your CLAUDE.md, skills, and hooks on every model release, and add back only what you observe the model repeatedly failing without. He is the one who builds the harness, which makes his dissent hard to dismiss.

Everyone agrees the feedback signal is what makes the loop work. Tan calls it the librarian and the contradiction checks, Wang calls it the eval or metric, Boris calls it verification. Nobody disagrees on that.

The disagreement is entirely about how much written structure surrounds the loop, and it is empirically testable on your own work. That is the gap worth building something in.
